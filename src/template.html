The model

What the café and the stays make possible

The café, the stays, the weekend happenings and the market earn a modest income. It goes back into the place and the people around it, and gives us room to keep trying things. It is one way of working among many, and we are still learning whether it holds.

What the earnings make possible

A team from the village

Salaries for the full-time team from the local community. Wages for women who manage their households and still come in for a few hours. Steady work for local repair and maintenance teams and suppliers as the campus grows.

A community kitchen

One kitchen that feeds everyone working at Kaaya, including the volunteers and interns who come to support community initiatives.

The smart-village centre

Honorariums for the two community volunteers who run the ISVC centre at Tilwari, where children and villagers gather for information and meetings. Kaaya also set up the children's library there, run by volunteers.

Days for the village, and its young people

Women's Day, Independence Day and Children's Day with the women and youth of the village. Internships for local youth, and fellowships such as Padav.

Still learning

Much of what happens here builds on what the village, government programmes and organisations working in these hills had already begun. We are grateful to learn alongside them, and we expect this model to keep changing as we understand more.

Coming back, and going deeper

The idea

A few people chose to live in a village, to see what would happen

That was 2011. The question is still open, and Kaaya keeps exploring it through how the campus is built, how it works with the village, and how it welcomes visitors.

Kaaya means body

In Sanskrit, kaaya is the body. The name keeps the work tangible: building with mud, growing food, eating together. It is also why the café and stays are called Kaaya Offline. You have to come and feel it.

Kaaya began as a rural–urban connect. Supermarkets, online shopping and phones have pulled the city and the village apart. Kaaya tries to make the exchange between them flow both ways, so each gains from the other.

It is run as a social enterprise, held for now by K-Green Life Pvt Ltd until a better institutional form emerges.

Built from what the land offered

No architects were hired. The walls are made of soil dug on site, by people from the village who know how to build with it. Mud breathes, keeping the rooms cool in summer and warm in winter. The huts follow the contours of the land, close enough to share, apart enough for quiet.

The newest building, the Annexe, joins traditional and modern methods, with local craftsmanship in its beams. Local soil, local skills and local knowledge kept the cost low.

A school camp pays local cooks and guides; a jar of pickle turns a farmer's surplus into income. Small exchanges like these keep some value close to home.

Expertise lives with the people who depend on it every day, like the dairy farmer who knows feed, costs and purity better than any textbook.

On learning

Students know the theory but have rarely handled living soil. Unstructured time, even boredom, has its place.

On living

In Tilwari, wellbeing depends on neighbours, animals and the land. Sustainability is a way of life before it is a project.

Community and climate

Twelve villages, one valley

Tilwari sits in the Doon Valley. Farming is getting harder to live from, and many young people are leaving. Kaaya works on this slowly, alongside the people who live here, and is still learning how.

The smart-village centre

Kaaya hosts the coordination node of the Setu Aayog Smart Village Centre programme for the Tilwari cluster. It brings together e-governance, banking, telemedicine, farming, tourism and watershed work in one place.

Two volunteers from the community run the centre, where children and villagers gather for information and meetings. Kaaya set up a children's library there, also run by volunteers.

Women, produce and the Sunday Market

Farmers and kitchen gardeners sell their surplus directly to neighbours at the Sunday Market. What does not sell is solar-dried, dehydrated or pickled. Under UMSVY, women's groups train at Kaaya in processing, packaging and branding.

Students map what households buy, to see where the village's money leaves for distant supply chains, and how more of it could stay.

Land and water

Climate-smart farming, recharging springs, poly-tunnels, and helping neighbours grow their kitchen gardens organically.

Days for the village

Through the year Kaaya holds days with the women and young people of the village: Women's Day, Independence Day, Children's Day.

Fellowships

A place for ideas that are still becoming

Ideas need time in real conditions before they face the world. Kaaya offers its campus, its village and its people as that place.

Padav

A mentoring fellowship for young artists from the remote Himalayan hills. An artist from Uttarakhand brings creative mentorship, networks and craft; Kaaya brings the campus, the logistics and the quiet. Fellows' work hangs in the Gallery at Kaaya Offline.

▶ Padav, June 2023: a short film by the fellows ↗

Anutraaya

An incubation that asks what happens to knowledge when the conditions that produced it disappear. It rethinks food, language and craft in hill life, practised in Tilwari rather than kept in a museum.

Internships and volunteers

Over the years, interns and volunteers from the village, from across India and from abroad have spent time here, in the garden, the kitchen, the café and the village. Their stories appear in Field Notes below.

Call for applications

Sustainable Living Fellowship

An invitation to spend three months living on the campus as a small team, learning to live more lightly by building, growing and making things with your hands. Kaaya provides food and a place to stay. People from the village and invited practitioners come in from time to time to work alongside the team.

Who it is for: people drawn to sustainable architecture, interior design, community living, or simply making and mending, especially those who like to repurpose, recycle and reuse. You do not need to be an expert; you need to want to learn by doing.

What the team might work on:

  • Finishing a mud house, with interiors made from reclaimed materials
  • Landscaping with plants native to these hills
  • Shaping shared living spaces on the campus

To apply: write to connect@kaaya.org with your CV and a short note about yourself and why this interests you.

When: applications are open on a rolling basis, with no fixed start or end date. Three months is the time we have found it takes to really settle in and live the place.

Learning Centre

Learning alongside

Experience, engage, express. Most of the learning here comes from the people who live it: the farmer, the potter, the cattle keeper, the women who grow and cook. Kaaya steps aside and lets the village teach, and we keep learning alongside the groups who come.

How learning happens here

The practitioner is the teacher

The person who leads the milk journey owns the cows. He knows what a cow eats, what it costs, how the milk is tested and what he is paid for it, because his living depends on it. Farmers are paid for sharing what they know.

From the classroom to the field

Students arrive having read and debated climate, food and sustainability. Here they find the ground that ties it together. Every journey mixes thinking, seeing and touching, and doing.

Letting nature teach

Forests, streams, smells and sounds, a little fear and a lot of delight. We value unstructured time too, and sometimes let a busy child be bored, because that is often where imagination comes back.

Starting from what is already there

Students use appreciative enquiry: looking first for the strengths a person or a village already has, and how an outsider might help build on them.

From consuming to making

In the maker's space young people upcycle and reuse, and make seed bombs for reforestation, so that a large problem comes with something they can do with their own hands.

A connection with roots

Supermarkets and screens keep the city and the village apart. Time here shows how village life quietly supports city life, and what each of us can do as a consumer.

The journeys

Know your food

The seed journey: ploughing with bullocks, levelling and furrowing, preparing manure, treating and sowing seed, and later harvesting, winnowing and grinding. How much science, wisdom and labour goes into the food we eat.

Know your milk

A day in the life of a dairy farmer: cutting and mixing fodder, feeding, washing and milking, testing the milk for fat, and how the village milk cooperative works.

Know your soil

The building blocks of mountains, rivers and forest, the rocks and soil we stand on, and the ecological history of the land.

Village walks, nature trails and making

Mud architecture, compost and kitchen gardens, biogas, water mills and water harvesting, with local guides; trails to read the forest; and pottery, craft and clay with village artisans.

The journeys touch six themes: water, forest and conservation; agriculture and food; dairy and cooperatives; craft and artisans; traditional knowledge; and rural society and the panchayat.

Three ways to come

Outdoor learning camps · 1 to 5 days

For schools: the food, milk and soil journeys in a working village, linked to what children study in class. Summer camps where city children make friends across schools.

Community projects · 1 to 5 weeks

Students choose a real local need and work on it in teams with the people concerned: listening, documenting, planning and making something together.

Live projects and internships · up to 6 months

Students and young professionals bring their own subject to a practical problem in the village, for an internship, a dissertation or a live project.

Institutions and teams

Workshops, exposure visits and partnerships, using the hall, the amphitheatre and the village.

What a community project can look like

A studio for the potter

Sushil Kumar comes from a family of potters. As plastic and metal replaced clay, the rest of his family left the craft, but he still loves it. Kaaya set aside a room on the campus as his studio, where he can work, sell and teach. Students meet his family, document how soil becomes a pot, and help design the space with the carpenter, while asking why pottery is growing in cities and fading in villages.

From kitchen garden to market

Women farmers trained in organic growing had a new problem: what to do with the surplus. Kaaya began buying it at market rates, with Sureshi Panwar weighing and paying on the spot. Students visit the farms, trace each step from field to shopping bag, learn pickle-making, and think about how city demand can reach the farmer directly. It grew into the Farmer's Market.

The people who guide

Learning at Kaaya is guided by people from the village and a small team who have run camps with children and young adults for many years. Sureshi Panwar, an educator and community coordinator from one of the neighbouring villages, takes children well beyond the textbook. Darshan Lal helps students link what they learn in books to real life around them. Alongside them are the farmers, cattle keepers and artisans who are the real teachers here.

The space

Mud cottages and a dormitory built from the site's own soil, a hall, an open-air amphitheatre, sit-outs and lawns, a kitchen garden, and the village all around. Forty minutes from Dehradun.
